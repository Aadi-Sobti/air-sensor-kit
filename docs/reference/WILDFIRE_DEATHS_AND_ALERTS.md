# Fatal US wildfires 2016–2026: did the alerts work, and could people get out or get help?

Researched 2026-11-26. Four researchers tried to cover every US wildfire from 2016 to November 2026 where civilians died; a final review found a few they missed (see Limits). A fact-checker
re-checked each one against its sources (official reports where they exist, otherwise news), and a final reviewer looked for gaps. It feeds the Home Beacon plan
(`docs/HOME_BEACON.md` §§ 2a and 8). Individual victims aren't named here.

## The short answer
**Yes. People died even in fires where an official alert went out, but in almost none of them is a timely alert documented as reaching the people who died.**
- **Where most people died, the official alert was late or patchy.** Across 49 fatal fires (366 civilian deaths), 79% of the deaths
  were in fires where the official alert came late or reached only some people. Only 1% (5 deaths in 4 fires) were in
  fires where a timely alert is documented (for Thomas, only inferred) as reaching the victims. The other 20% are unclear: the sources don't show whether an official alert reached
  the victims in time (in some, such as Palisades and McKinney, at least some victims knew of the danger).
- **Where a warning did reach people, they still died because they couldn't leave, or didn't:**
  - very old or disabled people who couldn't get out alone;
  - people who stayed to defend their home or went back for pets or keepsakes;
  - people whose warning came as the flames arrived.
- **Getting help failed again and again:**
  - 911 was swamped, and dispatchers had no one to send;
  - relatives were stopped at roadblocks;
  - rescuers couldn't reach blocked streets;
  - phones and power failed;
  - help that was ready was never called.
- **The people who died were mostly older, and many had disabilities.**
  - **Camp Fire:** mean age 72 (forensic study); the State Auditor counted 67 of 85 aged 65 or older.
  - **The 2025 LA fires:** all but 4 of 31 were over 65 or disabled, median age 76.
  - **Lahaina:** 72 of 99 identified victims were 60 or older.
- **Most died at home, or within a short distance of it.**
  - **Camp Fire:** about 59 of 83 were found inside their homes (a count from AP's summary of the DA's report).
  - **Lahaina:** at least 65 of 102 were trying to evacuate when the fire overtook them, on average only about
    800 ft from home.

## What went wrong, in three gaps
**1. The alert reached a phone, not the person**, or reached nobody:
- **Night and early-morning fires:** Tubbs and the other October 2017 fires (Napa's Grand Jury warned silent texts may not wake heavy sleepers), Concow in the Camp Fire (NIST lists people asleep), and west Altadena, whose first order came at 3:25 a.m.
- **Older people who don't watch phones:** LA County's review calls this a digital divide.
- **Loudspeaker warnings not heard:** in Lahaina they were lost in the wind, inside closed homes, or on people with
  hearing loss.
- **Opt-in systems most people never joined:**
  - **Camp Fire:** under 40% had signed up for CodeRED, and it reached about 17% of Paradise and Magalia.
  - **Tubbs:** under 2% enrolled, per a news count.
- **Phone-wide alerts (WEA) not sent, or failed:**
  - Butte County's attempt failed;
  - Sonoma chose not to use them;
  - Gatlinburg's request failed.
- **Phone, power and 911 failures:**
  - **October 2017 North Bay fires:** more than 70 cell towers lost (KQED, news).
  - **Lahaina:** power was out, and cell and landline service was intermittent that afternoon (fully down only from the next day).
  - **North Complex:** a planned power shutoff was in effect.

**2. The person couldn't leave alone, or left too late** (whether each was warned is mostly undocumented):
- **Limited mobility, no car, or unable to drive:**
  - **Camp Fire:** about 14–16 of 83 victim summaries record this.
  - **Carr:** a 70-year-old with no car died at home with two great-grandchildren; the family had not been ordered to evacuate when the fire whirl hit.
- **Garage doors that wouldn't open without power:** Camp Fire, Tubbs.
- **Caregivers missing or unable to help:**
  - **Eaton:** a disabled father and son died while their usual caregiver was in hospital.
  - **Palisades:** a mother with a broken arm couldn't lift her disabled son.
- **Leaving too late, then overtaken:**
  - **Camp Fire:** NIST counted at least 19 burnovers.
  - **Lahaina:** gridlock, blocked roads and locked gates.
  - **Palisades Drive:** gridlock and abandoned cars blocked the road (no deaths there are documented).

**3. They couldn't get help:**
- **911 overwhelmed:**
  - **Lahaina:** dispatchers repeatedly told callers there was no one to send.
  - **Camp Fire:** a mobile-home park resident told 911 they had no way to evacuate, and a caller asking for help there was told no one was available (SF Chronicle).
  - **Eaton:** when the disabled father and son called, the dispatcher said there were many calls in that area.
- **Family blocked:** relatives were turned back at roadblocks (Carr, Eaton).
- **Rescuers couldn't get in:** Gatlinburg's hardest-hit areas were unreachable until morning.
- **Help existed but wasn't used:**
  - **Eaton:** the county's paratransit service was ready by 4 a.m. but never called.
  - **California:** in 2019 the State Auditor found none of three counties reviewed (Butte, Sonoma, Ventura) had
    assessed which residents would need help to evacuate, or planned that help in advance.

**When an order did reach people, almost all of them left.** In UC surveys after the 2017–2018 California fires,
87–97% of people who got a mandatory order evacuated. The most common reason people stayed was that no order reached
them. (The surveys are small, online and self-selected.)

## What this means for the beacon
- **A loud in-home alarm addresses the first gap:** the alert reaching a phone but not a sleeping or older person. It
  only helps if an alert exists in time, though. It can't fix a late official order. Fire Watch's own confirmed-fire
  alerts could come earlier: in west Altadena, fire was reported hours before the order. But that depends on Fire
  Watch confirming fires quickly and correctly, which isn't proven yet.
- **The second gap, people who can't leave alone, is where a household's advance information matters most.** Know
  *before* a fire who will need help to leave (mobility, no car, caregiver). Help like paratransit can then be sent at
  the warning stage, not after the order. This is the strongest case for the partner view (`docs/HOME_BEACON.md`
  § 8). It's also exactly what the State Auditor found counties lacked.
- **For the third gap, "I need help" must reach someone who can act.** 911 and family both failed in these fires. An
  "I'm OK" press that takes a home off the check list frees responders for the homes that need them.
- **Practical tips for the guide** (founder decides the wording):
  - how to release a garage door by hand when the power is out;
  - leave at the warning stage if anyone in the home can't leave quickly;
  - don't stay to defend with a garden hose.
- **What the beacon can't fix:** people who decide to stay. The household circle can see that they haven't checked in.

## Limits of this research
- **Per-victim alert receipt** is rarely documented. Most grades describe the area, not each person.
- **Death counts involve judgment calls.** Sources disagree for several fires (Carr 4 fire deaths vs 7–8 in official totals, Tubbs 22 vs 24, Sand 1 vs 2, South Fork 2 vs 3), and the 366 includes a dust-storm crash (Oklahoma), an ATV crash (Brewer) and a death whose cause wasn't finalized (Gann).
- **Primary reports not read in full:** NIST's full Camp Fire study (too large to fetch), the 724-page UL/FSRI report on the 2025 LA fires (blocked; known only from news summaries), the Butte County DA's Camp Fire report (blocked; per-victim data came from AP's summary), the Black Saturday Royal
  Commission, and parts of the Gatlinburg and Oregon reviews. Some numbers come from news or from summaries of
  official reports; the research labels these.
- **The fire list is incomplete.** The final review found civilian deaths the researchers missed: Saddleridge (Los Angeles, 2019, 1 death, confirmed by LAFD), Mountain View (Mono County, 2020, 1) and Substation (Oregon, 2018, 1), and possibly 2 in the 2023 Louisiana fires. Plains fires such as the 2017 Southern Plains fires and the 2018 Rhea Fire were found but not researched. There is no central US list of civilian wildfire deaths.
- **Other countries** (Black Saturday 2009, Pedrógão Grande 2017, Mati 2018) were found but not verified from their
  official inquiries.
- **The full structured results** (every fire, every source, every fact-check correction) are in the research run's
  output. Ask for any fire's details.
